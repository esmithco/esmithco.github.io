Math background
  • * Let \(y_t\) be the studied event, observed at time \(t\).
  • * \(y_t\) is a random variable — it has a probability distribution.
  • * \(\mathrm{I}\) is the information we have. So what we actually forecast is \(y_t \mid \mathrm{I}\) — \(y_t\) given \(\mathrm{I}\).
  • * The point forecast is \(E(y_t \mid \mathrm{I})\), the distribution's mean.
  • * The interval comes from the standard deviation, \(\sigma = \sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(y_t \mid \mathrm{I})}\) — not the variance itself, since variance sits in squared units and can't share \(y_t\)'s scale. The interval is point forecast \(\pm\, z\sigma\).
mean → point forecast · σ → interval width
How many σ, for how much probability
Not linear — it follows the normal curve, via the z-score.

So \(\pm 0.9\sigma\) is about 63%, not 90% — the decimals lining up is a coincidence, not a rule.
